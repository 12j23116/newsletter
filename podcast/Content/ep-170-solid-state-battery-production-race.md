## [SEGMENT 1: Why Solid-State Batteries Matter]

**Dr. Sarah Chen:** Jagdeep, let's start with the basics. What makes solid-state batteries different from the lithium-ion batteries that are in most EVs today?

**Jagdeep Singh:** The fundamental difference is the electrolyte. In a conventional lithium-ion battery, the electrolyte is a liquid — it's a flammable organic solvent that allows lithium ions to move between the anode and cathode during charging and discharging. In a solid-state battery, the electrolyte is a solid — in our case, a ceramic. That has several important consequences. First, safety — a solid electrolyte can't catch fire the way a liquid can. Second, energy density — a solid electrolyte enables the use of a pure lithium metal anode, which stores much more energy per unit volume than the graphite anodes used in conventional batteries. And third, fast charging — the solid electrolyte can handle higher current densities, which means faster charging without degradation.

**Dr. James Park:** And 500 Wh/kg — how does that compare to what's on the road today?

**Jagdeep Singh:** The best production EV batteries today are around 250 to 300 Wh/kg. So 500 Wh/kg is roughly double. In practical terms, that means either doubling the range of an EV at the same battery weight, or halving the battery weight for the same range. An EV that currently gets 300 miles of range could get 600 miles with a 500 Wh/kg battery at the same weight. Or you could keep 300 miles of range and cut the battery weight by 200 to 300 kilograms, which would improve efficiency, handling, and cost.

**Dr. Sarah Chen:** What about cycle life? One of the concerns with lithium metal anodes has been dendrite growth — these lithium filaments that grow through the electrolyte and cause short circuits. Has that been solved?

**Jagdeep Singh:** That's been the central technical challenge, and it's what's taken the industry decades to solve. The dendrite problem is why solid-state batteries have been "five years away" for twenty years. What we've developed is a ceramic separator — it's a proprietary oxide ceramic material — that has high enough mechanical strength to resist dendrite penetration while still allowing lithium ions to pass through. We've demonstrated over 1,000 charge-discharge cycles at 500 Wh/kg with less than 5 percent capacity loss. That's well beyond what's needed for a consumer EV, which typically needs about 800 cycles to last the life of the vehicle.

## [SEGMENT 2: The Manufacturing Challenge]

**Dr. James Park:** Let's talk about manufacturing. You've proven the cell works. How do you go from lab-scale cells to mass production?

**Jagdeep Singh:** This is where the rubber meets the road, as they say. The challenge with solid-state batteries is that the ceramic electrolyte is a new material that doesn't fit into existing battery manufacturing lines. Conventional lithium-ion batteries are made using a roll-to-roll process — you coat the electrodes, wind them into a jelly roll, inject the liquid electrolyte, and seal it. Our process is fundamentally different because you're working with a ceramic, which requires different handling, different deposition techniques, and different quality control.

**Dr. Sarah Chen:** What's your approach? Are you building your own factories or partnering with existing manufacturers?

**Jagdeep Singh:** We're doing both. We've built our own pilot production line in San Jose, which is currently producing cells at a rate of about 50,000 cells per year. That's enough for validation testing with automotive partners. For volume production, we're partnering with an established battery manufacturer — we've announced our partnership with PowerCo, Volkswagen's battery subsidiary. They'll be building the gigafactory-scale production lines using our technology and our process know-how. The target is to begin volume production in 2027.

**Dr. James Park:** What are the specific manufacturing challenges you've had to solve?

**Jagdeep Singh:** The biggest one is the ceramic separator. You need to produce a thin — about 20 to 30 microns — ceramic layer that's uniform, defect-free, and can be produced at high throughput. We use a tape casting process, which is similar to how ceramic substrates are made for the electronics industry, but we've had to adapt it for much thinner layers and much tighter quality requirements. A single pinhole in the ceramic can lead to a dendrite short, so the defect rate has to be extraordinarily low. We've gotten it down to parts-per-billion levels, which is comparable to semiconductor manufacturing standards.

**Dr. Sarah Chen:** What about cost? Solid-state batteries have historically been much more expensive than conventional lithium-ion.

**Jagdeep Singh:** At low volumes, yes. But at scale, we believe the cost can be competitive. The ceramic material itself is not expensive — it's made from relatively abundant raw materials. The cost is in the process — the equipment, the yield, the throughput. As we scale up and improve yields, the cost comes down. Our target is to be at parity with conventional lithium-ion on a cost-per-kilowatt-hour basis by 2028 or 2029. And when you factor in that a solid-state battery pack can be smaller and lighter for the same range, the system-level cost could actually be lower.

## [SEGMENT 3: The Competitive Landscape]

**Dr. James Park:** QuantumScape isn't the only company pursuing solid-state batteries. You've got Toyota, Samsung SDI, SES, Solid Power, and others all working on this. How do you view the competition?

**Jagdeep Singh:** I think it's a validation of the technology that so many serious companies are pursuing it. The approaches differ — some companies are using sulfide-based electrolytes, some are using oxides like us, some are using polymers. Each has trade-offs. Sulfides have high ionic conductivity but are sensitive to moisture and can generate toxic gases. Oxides are more stable but harder to manufacture. Polymers are easy to process but have lower energy density. We chose the oxide route because we believe it offers the best combination of safety, performance, and manufacturability, even though the manufacturing challenge is significant.

**Dr. Sarah Chen:** What about the Chinese battery makers? CATL and BYD are dominant in conventional lithium-ion. Are they pursuing solid-state as well?

**Jagdeep Singh:** Yes, very actively. CATL has announced a solid-state program targeting production by 2027 or 2028. The Chinese government has made solid-state batteries a national priority, and there's significant funding flowing into the sector. I think the competition will be intense, but I also think the market is large enough — and growing fast enough — that there's room for multiple players. The global EV battery market is projected to be over 300 billion dollars by 2030.

**Dr. James Park:** What about beyond EVs? Are there other applications for solid-state batteries?

**Jagdeep Singh:** Absolutely. Aviation is a huge opportunity — the energy density improvement is even more critical for aircraft than for cars. Electric vertical takeoff and landing aircraft, or eVTOLs, need very high energy density to be viable, and 500 Wh/kg is in the range that makes short-range electric aviation possible. We're also looking at consumer electronics — laptops and phones with double the battery life — and grid storage, though the cost requirements there are more demanding.
